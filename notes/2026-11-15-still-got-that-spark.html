Still Got That Spark

Notes on the sermon Pastor Sam preached at the first Sunday service of IFGF Bandung on 15 November 2026, a message in the November series “Dreams and Visions”. The main text is Joshua 14:6-14, where Caleb, at 85, asks for the hill country of Hebron that God had promised him 45 years earlier. The sermon was preached in Indonesian with many English lines; these notes are in English and keep his English sayings word for word, with his Indonesian beside them where it helps. Every Bible passage is quoted in full: in the English Standard Version (ESV) for English, and in the Indonesian Terjemahan Baru (TB) for the passages he read aloud in Indonesian. Where he read from another English version, the New International Version (NIV) or The Message (MSG), that version is quoted and marked as the one he read, with the ESV beside it. Each chapter opens with a four-cell chain (problem, big idea, the tools he uses, result), then tells the argument in plain words, draws it in a figure, and sets out the details: his definitions, his Bible examples, his stories and the reasoning that joins them. A few short boxes headed “Reading it carefully” compare a detail of the sermon with the wording of the text. Times in brackets, such as (1:16:56), point to the moment in the livestream recording.

The whole sermon: Caleb's spark at 85

ProblemMany believers still work, still go to church and still keep busy, yet something is missing: the spark has gone out.
Big ideaA God-given vision keeps the spark alive at every age, as it did for Caleb, who at 85 still asked for the mountain God had promised.
How (tools)Caleb's story (Numbers 13 to 14, Joshua 14:6-14) and three marks of a lasting spark: Excitement, Expectation and Experience.
ResultPassion that comes from the inside out, hope that trusts God's character, and action that takes part in God's work, passed on to the next generation.
In plain words

The sermon starts from a picture everyone recognises. Some people walk into a room and the room lights up; others walk in and a cloud seems to settle over it. Both may be doing the same things, going to work, going to church, meeting people, but one of them still has a spark and the other does not. Pastor Sam's title, “Still got that spark”, which he renders in Indonesian as masih nyala (still alight) and masih ngotot, masih mau, masih ingin (still insistent, still willing, still wanting), names what he wants his hearers to keep.

His model is Caleb. At 40 Caleb was one of the twelve spies Moses sent into Canaan, and one of only two who said “we can”. Because the people believed the other ten, Israel spent forty years in the wilderness. Forty-five years after the spying, when the land was being divided, Caleb came to Joshua and said that he was as strong at 85 as he had been at 40, and asked for Hebron, the hill country where the giants lived. Pastor Sam's reading is that this strength was more than muscle: it was strength of vision, memory of God's promise and determination. What set Caleb apart, he says, was not that he wanted land but that he “followed the Lord wholeheartedly” and had “a different spirit”.

Because the series is about dreams and visions, he first sets a boundary. God still gives dreams and visions, but believers are called to follow God, not dreams and visions, and the complete Bible already tells them what to do. With that in place he names three marks of a spark that lasts, each with its own tagline. Excitement: “Vision awakens our passion.” Expectation: “Vision sustains our resilience.” Experience: “Vision inspires our participation.” The three are built in order. Without excitement there is no expectation, and excitement alone is not enough; without action, neither excitement nor expectation comes to anything. He closes by setting Moses, who saw the land but did not enter it, beside Caleb, who entered and received Hebron, and draws the conclusion that God's promises cross generations, so the spark is something to pass on to one's children.

The central claim

Put in one sentence: a God-given vision keeps a believer's spark alive at every age by awakening real excitement, sustaining an expectation that trusts God's character, and moving the believer to act, as Caleb did when at 85 he still asked for the mountain God had promised.

PART ATHE STORY BEHIND THE TEXT
Part A · Numbers 13 to 14

Twelve spies, two reports: the back story

ProblemIsrael stood at the edge of the promised land, and ten of the twelve spies said it could not be taken.
Big ideaAll twelve saw the same good land and the same giants; what differed was whether they believed God was with them.
How (tools)Numbers 13 to 14 retold: the spies, the fruit, the two reports, God's anger and the forty years; then a short sketch of the conquest in Joshua.
ResultOnly Joshua and Caleb of the old generation would enter, which sets up Caleb's request in Joshua 14.
In plain words

Pastor Sam tells the back story briefly, noting that long-time Christians will already know it (0:52:42). In the book of Numbers, Moses sent twelve spies (pengintai) into the promised land to find out what was in it. All twelve came back agreeing that the land was good: it flowed with milk and honey, it was fertile, and they even brought back a cluster of grapes so large it had to be carried between several men. But ten of them added that there were giants there and that the cities were fortified. Their conclusion was “we can't, we can't, we can't”. Joshua and Caleb said “we can, we can” (0:53:26 to 0:53:30).

He is fair to the ten. These were people just out of slavery, so it is natural that they were intimidated by such large things (0:53:19). The point is not that the ten saw wrongly. They saw the same land, the same fruit and the same giants as Joshua and Caleb. The difference lay in what each group concluded, and that difference is what the sermon will later call Caleb's “different spirit”.

The result was that God was angry at Israel's unbelief. The people wandered about 38 years, then spent about two years preparing to enter, so that they were 40 years in the wilderness in all. When the time came, they again sent spies, crossed the Jordan, and under Joshua attacked “from here and from there”. They took almost all of the land, but, he notes, Scripture says plainly that they did not finish the conquest. Then the land was divided as an inheritance (milik pusaka), and this is where Joshua 14 begins (0:53:33 to 0:54:20).

What Numbers says about the two reports

The passages below are quoted in the English Standard Version (ESV). The ten spies did not deny that the land was good; they set the danger beside it, and Caleb answered them directly:

27And they told him, “We came to the land to which you sent us. It flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit. 28However, the people who dwell in the land are strong, and the cities are fortified and very large. And besides, we saw the descendants of Anak there. 29The Amalekites dwell in the land of the Negeb. The Hittites, the Jebusites, and the Amorites dwell in the hill country. And the Canaanites dwell by the sea, and along the Jordan.” 30But Caleb quieted the people before Moses and said, “Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it.” 31Then the men who had gone up with him said, “We are not able to go up against the people, for they are stronger than we are.” 32So they brought to the people of Israel a bad report of the land that they had spied out, saying, “The land, through which we have gone to spy it out, is a land that devours its inhabitants, and all the people that we saw in it are of great height. 33And there we saw the Nephilim (the sons of Anak, who come from the Nephilim), and we seemed to ourselves like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them.”

Numbers 13:27-33 · ESV

Joshua and Caleb then spoke together to the whole congregation. Their report rests on one condition, God's favour, and one reason, that the LORD is with them:

6And Joshua the son of Nun and Caleb the son of Jephunneh, who were among those who had spied out the land, tore their clothes 7and said to all the congregation of the people of Israel, “The land, which we passed through to spy it out, is an exceedingly good land. 8If the LORD delights in us, he will bring us into this land and give it to us, a land that flows with milk and honey. 9Only do not rebel against the LORD. And do not fear the people of the land, for they are bread for us. Their protection is removed from them, and the LORD is with us; do not fear them.”

Numbers 14:6-9 · ESV
Reading it carefully: He says the grape cluster took “several people” to carry (0:53:08). The text says “they carried it on a pole between two of them” (Numbers 13:23, ESV). The point, that the land's fruit was unmistakably rich, is unchanged.

Forty years, about 38 of them after Kadesh

His count, “about 38 years” of wandering and “two years” of preparation, 40 years in all (0:53:36 to 0:53:41), matches the two texts that give the numbers. God set the total at forty years, a year for each day of spying:

33And your children shall be shepherds in the wilderness forty years and shall suffer for your faithlessness, until the last of your dead bodies lies in the wilderness. 34According to the number of the days in which you spied out the land, forty days, a year for each day, you shall bear your iniquity forty years, and you shall know my displeasure.'

Numbers 14:33-34 · ESV

14And the time from our leaving Kadesh-barnea until we crossed the brook Zered was thirty-eight years, until the entire generation, that is, the men of war, had perished from the camp, as the LORD had sworn to them.

Deuteronomy 2:14 · ESV

Only two of the old generation

He stresses that out of the millions who could have entered, only two of the old generation did: Joshua and Caleb (0:55:26). God's sentence in Numbers names them as the exceptions:

30not one shall come into the land where I swore that I would make you dwell, except Caleb the son of Jephunneh and Joshua the son of Nun.

Numbers 14:30 · ESV

From the wilderness to the division of the land

He passes quickly over the book of Joshua: spies are sent again, the people cross the Jordan, and the land is largely taken but not completely. Both points are in the text. Joshua sent two spies to Jericho, and late in Joshua's life God tells him that much land remains:

1And Joshua the son of Nun sent two men secretly from Shittim as spies, saying, “Go, view the land, especially Jericho.” And they went and came into the house of a prostitute whose name was Rahab and lodged there.

Joshua 2:1 · ESV

1Now Joshua was old and advanced in years, and the LORD said to him, “You are old and advanced in years, and there remains yet very much land to possess.

Joshua 13:1 · ESV
Reading it carefully: At the start he says “wherever you set your foot, it becomes an inheritance” (0:52:34), inside the Numbers retelling. That wording is not in Numbers 13 to 14. The general promise to Israel is “Every place on which the sole of your foot treads shall be yours” (Deuteronomy 11:24, ESV; also Joshua 1:3), and the specific promise to Caleb is “the land on which your foot has trodden” (Joshua 14:9, ESV), meaning the Hebron area he walked as a spy. The texts say the land becomes Israel's or Caleb's possession. His point, that the spies walked land God was giving, still stands.

The reasoning, step by step

  1. Same factsAll twelve spies agreed the land was good and that giants and walled cities were there.
  2. Different conclusionsTen said “we are not able”; Joshua and Caleb said “we are well able”, because “the LORD is with us”.
  3. ConsequencesThe people sided with the ten, God was angry at their unbelief, and the generation died in the wilderness over 40 years.
  4. The exceptionOnly Joshua and Caleb of that generation would enter, so Caleb carries a promise across those years.

Part A · Joshua 14:6-14

“Give me this hill country”: Caleb's request at 85

ProblemAt 85, after 45 years of waiting, Caleb could have asked for an easy portion or for nothing at all.
Big ideaHe asked for the hardest place, the giants' hill country, because he remembered God's promise and still had the strength of a man who followed God wholeheartedly.
How (tools)Joshua 14:6-14 read in the Indonesian Terjemahan Baru (TB) with Pastor Sam's comments, set beside the English Standard Version (ESV).
ResultCaleb's “strength” means vision, memory of the promise and determination, and he received Hebron because he wholly followed the LORD.
In plain words

Pastor Sam reads the main text from the Terjemahan Baru (TB), almost word for word, stopping three times to comment. The scene is the division of the land. The people of Judah come to Joshua at Gilgal, and Caleb reminds Joshua of what the LORD said to Moses about the two of them at Kadesh-barnea. He tells his story: he was 40 when he was sent, he brought back an honest report, the others made the people's hearts melt, but he followed the LORD wholeheartedly. Moses swore that the land his foot had walked on would be his and his children's for ever.

Then comes the line the sermon is built on. God has kept Caleb alive 45 years; he is 85; and he is as strong now as when Moses sent him. Pastor Sam's first comment is that this strength is “not physical only” (bukannya secara fisikal saja): Caleb is as strong in his vision, in his memory of God's promise, and in his determination to fulfil God's call. He sums it up in English: “It's not talking about physical” (0:56:06 to 0:56:27).

Caleb then asks for the hill country where the Anakim live, with their great fortified cities: “It may be that the LORD will be with me, and I shall drive them out just as the LORD said.” Pastor Sam's second comment is that this may not be written down anywhere, but God had evidently told Caleb he would defeat the giants of Hebron, so Caleb says, in effect, “I want to see that happen; even at 85 I still want to defeat giants” (0:56:45 to 0:56:57). Joshua blesses him and gives him Hebron. The third comment asks why Caleb insisted on Hebron. He admits he does not know, but notes that Hebron is still a very special place for Jews, because Abraham, Sarah, Isaac and Jacob are buried there in the cave of Machpelah. Caleb's attitude is: “I want Hebron, I remember God's promise, I will defeat those giants” (0:57:18 to 0:57:35).

Yosua 14:6-9 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)

Reading verse 9, Pastor Sam paraphrases Moses' oath as “Caleb, because you brought good news, you will be given a reward” (0:55:09 to 0:55:14). He then adds that of the millions who could enter the land, only two of the old generation did (0:55:26).

Reading it carefully: The verse gives the reason for the oath as “because you have wholly followed the LORD my God” (Joshua 14:9, ESV). Caleb's honest report, “as it was in my heart” (14:7), was the evidence of that wholehearted following rather than the reason itself. Pastor Sam lands in the same place later, when he says that what set Caleb apart was that he followed the Lord wholeheartedly (see the spark).

Joshua 14:10-12 · ESV

10Jadi sekarang, sesungguhnya TUHAN telah memelihara hidupku, seperti yang dijanjikan-Nya. Kini sudah empat puluh lima tahun lamanya, sejak diucapkan TUHAN firman itu kepada Musa, dan selama itu orang Israel mengembara di padang gurun. Jadi sekarang, telah berumur delapan puluh lima tahun aku hari ini; 11pada waktu ini aku masih sama kuat seperti pada waktu aku disuruh Musa; seperti kekuatanku pada waktu itu demikianlah kekuatanku sekarang untuk berperang dan untuk keluar masuk. 12Oleh sebab itu, berikanlah kepadaku pegunungan, yang dijanjikan TUHAN pada waktu itu, sebab engkau sendiri mendengar pada waktu itu, bahwa di sana ada orang Enak dengan kota-kota yang besar dan berkubu. Mungkin TUHAN menyertai aku, sehingga aku menghalau mereka, seperti yang difirmankan TUHAN.”

Yosua 14:10-12 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)
Reading it carefully: He says “It's not talking about physical” (0:56:27), but just before that he put it as “not physical only” (bukannya secara fisikal saja, 0:56:11). Verse 11 does measure Caleb's strength physically, “for war and for going and coming”. The inner strength he draws out is well grounded in the same passage, which repeats three times that Caleb “wholly followed” the LORD (14:8, 9, 14) and has him rest his request on “just as the LORD said” (14:12). Read as “not only physical”, his point stands with the text.
Reading it carefully: On the giants he hedges: “maybe it is not written” (mungkin gak tertulis, 0:56:45). The recorded promises to Caleb are about the land for him and his descendants: “I will bring into the land into which he went, and his descendants shall possess it” (Numbers 14:24, ESV). Verse 12 ends “just as the LORD said”, and Joshua 15:14 records the outcome: “And Caleb drove out from there the three sons of Anak, Sheshai and Ahiman and Talmai, the descendants of Anak” (ESV). His caution is accurate, and the outcome he points to did happen.

Yosua 14:13-14 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)

Gilgal, the camp

Introducing verse 6, he explains that Gilgal was the centre of Israel's encampment, the place where they gathered, near Jericho (0:54:22 to 0:54:33).

Reading it carefully: He places Gilgal “near Jericho, on the mountains of Jericho” (di atas pegunungan Jericho, 0:54:33). The text puts it in the low Jordan valley: “they encamped at Gilgal on the east border of Jericho” (Joshua 4:19, ESV). “Near Jericho” is right; “on the mountains” is not. Nothing in the sermon depends on it.

Why Hebron

Pastor Sam says honestly that he does not know why Caleb insisted on Hebron (saya gak tau kenapa dia ngotot minta Hebron, 0:57:18). The reason he offers is its history: it is the burial place of the patriarchs, and it is still special to Jews today. Genesis records the first burial there and the last of the patriarchs:

19After this, Abraham buried Sarah his wife in the cave of the field of Machpelah east of Mamre (that is, Hebron) in the land of Canaan.

Genesis 23:19 · ESV

13for his sons carried him to the land of Canaan and buried him in the cave of the field at Machpelah, to the east of Mamre, which Abraham bought with the field from Ephron the Hittite to possess as a burying place.

Genesis 50:13 · ESV (the burial of Jacob)

Abraham and Isaac were buried in the same cave (Genesis 25:9; 49:31), so his list of Abraham, Sarah, Isaac and Jacob is accurate; Genesis 49:31 adds that Rebekah and Leah were buried there too. Numbers also shows that Hebron was the very place Caleb had walked through as a spy, where the sons of Anak lived, which fits the promise of “the land on which your foot has trodden” (Numbers 13:22, ESV: “They went up into the Negeb and came to Hebron. Ahiman, Sheshai, and Talmai, the descendants of Anak, were there.”).

The reasoning, step by step

  1. The promiseAt 40 Caleb followed the LORD wholeheartedly, and Moses swore that the land he walked would be his and his children's.
  2. The waitGod kept him alive 45 years, through the wilderness and the conquest, just as He said.
  3. The strengthAt 85 he is as strong as at 40. Because the passage keeps tying him to God's word and his wholehearted following, Pastor Sam reads this as strength of vision, memory and determination, not only of body.
  4. The requestBecause he remembers the promise, he asks for the very hill country God spoke of, giants and all, trusting that the LORD will be with him.
  5. The outcomeJoshua blesses him and gives him Hebron, and the text gives the reason a third time: he wholly followed the LORD.

PART BTHE SPARK
Part B · The title and its boundary

Still got that spark: what it is and where it comes from

ProblemPeople keep up the same activities, yet some bring life into a room and others bring a cloud; something can be missing even in a busy, churchgoing life.
Big ideaThe spark comes from following God, not from chasing dreams, prophecies or signs; Caleb's spark came from his wholehearted following and his “different spirit”.
How (tools)The image of eyes and rooms; Joel 2:28 and Acts 2:17 on dreams and visions; the Bible as complete; Numbers 14:24 on what set Caleb apart.
ResultA clear starting point for the three marks: vision is tied to God and His word, and it shows itself in those who obey what they already know.
In plain words

Having read Joshua 14, Pastor Sam gives the title: “Still got that spark”. In Indonesian he renders it masih nyala, still alight, and then piles up near-synonyms: masih ngotot, masih mau, masih ingin, masih nyala, still insistent, still willing, still wanting, still burning (0:57:37 to 0:57:49). The easiest place to see a spark, he says, is in the eyes. Some eyes blaze; some look happy and excited. It is often said that the eyes are the window of the heart, and from someone's eyes we can tell whether they are enthusiastic, passionate, full of aspirations they want to bring out (0:57:55 to 0:58:22).

He widens the picture from eyes to rooms. People often do the same activities, but you can tell from their body language, their eyes and the way they speak whether they have a spark, and whether they bring a positive atmosphere into a room. Some walk into a room and it becomes charged up, alight; others walk in and the room turns gloomy, as if a black cloud hung in the middle of it. Many people are still working, still going to church, still active from Monday to Saturday, still leaving the house and meeting people. The question is whether something is missing: is there still a spark, a flame, a fire? (0:58:24 to 0:59:19)

Because the series is called “Dreams and Visions”, the obvious answer would be: wait for God to give you a dream. He closes that door before going further. God does give direction through dreams and visions throughout Scripture, and He still can; that is His promise in Joel and Acts. But believers do not have to wait for a dream, a prophecy or sign after sign before doing anything. They have the Bible, which is complete, and in it they can read God's visions and dreams every day. “We are called to follow God, and not dreams and visions” (kita dipanggil untuk mengikuti Tuhan dan bukan mimpi dan penglihatan, 1:00:49). Then he turns back to Caleb. What set Caleb apart from millions of others, and from the ten spies, was not his desire for land but that he followed the Lord wholeheartedly and had a different spirit.

God still gives dreams and visions

He opens the series by noting that God speaks of dreams and visions throughout Scripture, and gives them not only to people who are senior in the faith. He gave them to Joseph, whom he calls “a small child”, and to Daniel, a very wise man; the New Testament even has a whole book of “visions from God, revelations from God”, Revelation (Wahyu) (0:51:47 to 0:52:06). Later he gives a roll call: God gave direction by dreams and visions to Jacob, to Joseph, even to Paul; He gave Jonah a command (perintah); He gave visions to Ezekiel, Isaiah and Jeremiah (0:59:24 to 0:59:44). Two of these, in the English Standard Version (ESV):

17As for these four youths, God gave them learning and skill in all literature and wisdom, and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams.

Daniel 1:17 · ESV

9And a vision appeared to Paul in the night: a man of Macedonia was standing there, urging him and saying, “Come over to Macedonia and help us.”

Acts 16:9 · ESV

Joseph was a young man rather than a small child when he dreamed: “Joseph, being seventeen years old, was pasturing the flock with his brothers” (Genesis 37:2, ESV). The point, that God gives dreams to the young as well as the old, is the same.

Is there still prophecy today? Yes, he answers. Can God still give dreams and visions? “That is His promise”, in Joel and in Acts: the old generation and the young generation will receive visions and dreams (0:59:47 to 1:00:03).

28“And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions.

Joel 2:28 · ESV

But do not be hyper-spiritual

The qualification follows straight away, and the rest of the sermon depends on it. Believers do not always have to wait for a dream, or wait for a prophet to prophesy over them, before they do something. They do not need sign after sign after sign, as in “if the sky is pink tomorrow, then it is from God” (1:00:03 to 1:00:21). God still gives visions and dreams, but now there is the Bible, God's word, in which visions and dreams from God can be read every day, “because the Bible is actually complete” (karena Alkitab sebenarnya sudah komplit, 1:00:36).

So he does not want his hearers to be “too hyper-spiritual”, needing at every turn to hear an audible voice before they move. “In my opinion God doesn't work like that” (1:00:38 to 1:00:47). The positive rule is short:

What he says to do

  • Follow God: “We are called to follow God, and not dreams and visions” (1:00:49).
  • Read the Bible: “If there are no dreams and visions yet, there is the Bible” (1:01:01).
  • Obey it simply: “What the Bible says you may do, do. What the Bible says you may not do, do not do. Very simple” (Apa yang Alkitab bilang boleh, lakukan. Apa yang Alkitab bilang tidak boleh, jangan lakukan. Simple sekali, 1:01:05).

What he warns against

  • Waiting for a dream before doing anything.
  • Waiting for a prophecy: “a prophet is here, we wait to be prophesied over”.
  • Sign after sign: “if the sky is pink tomorrow, it is from God”.
  • Needing an audible voice before moving.

The promise attached is that as believers walk in this obedience, “dreams and visions will realize themselves”, as they do what they already know of God (1:01:12 to 1:01:18). This is why, in the chapters that follow, vision is never a private feeling: it is always tied to God's purpose and God's word.

What set Caleb apart

He then returns to the text and asks his hearers to read Numbers at home. Caleb's focus was not the land. Yes, he clearly insisted on Hebron; “that may have been his dream, that may have been his aspiration, there is nothing wrong with that” (1:01:32 to 1:01:36). But Numbers says plainly what distinguished Caleb from millions of other people and from the ten spies: “he followed the Lord wholeheartedly” (dia mengikuti Tuhan dengan sepenuh hatinya); he had a “different spirit” (roh yang berbeda), a different heart (hati yang berbeda) (1:01:38 to 1:01:59). This is the verse he means, quoted here in the ESV and in the Indonesian Terjemahan Baru (TB):

Bilangan 14:24 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)

Numbers repeats the point when it names the only two men of the Exodus generation who would see the land:

Numbers 32:11-12 · ESV

From this he draws the line that joins Caleb at 40 to Caleb at 85. Because his following was wholehearted, at 85 he says “go forward, go in” (mari kita maju, mari kita masuk), just as at 40 he said “come on, we can, we can” (1:02:04 to 1:02:09). The spark was the same spark, and its source was the same.

Reading it carefully: He mentions in passing that “the eyes are the window of the heart”, introduced with “it is said” (dikatakan demikian kan, 0:58:07), so as a common saying rather than as a verse. It is a proverb, not a Bible text. The nearest verse, “The eye is the lamp of the body” (Matthew 6:22, ESV), makes a different point. Nothing in his argument rests on it.

The reasoning, step by step

  1. The observationBusy, churchgoing people can still lack a spark, and it shows in their eyes and in the rooms they enter.
  2. The tempting answerIn a series on dreams and visions, one might wait for God to send a dream or a sign.
  3. The correctionGod does still give dreams and visions, but believers are called to follow God, and the complete Bible already tells them what to do.
  4. The evidence from CalebNumbers names his wholehearted following and different spirit as what set him apart, not his wish for land.
  5. The conclusionThe spark is the fruit of following God, which is why it was as bright at 85 as at 40. The three marks that follow describe how it shows.

PART CTHREE MARKS OF A LASTING SPARK
Part C · Point 1 · Excitement

Excitement: “Vision awakens our passion”

ProblemWithout excitement, life turns into routine, people drag their feet, grow bored, and “nothing matters anymore”, which can lead into sin.
Big ideaA vision from God awakens passion in anyone, whatever their personality, because it comes from God's call and shows from the inside out.
How (tools)Psalm 122:1, Psalm 34:5, 2 Corinthians 3:16-18 and Moses at 120 as positive examples; Elijah and the older David as warnings; Joseph and young David; stories from his own life.
ResultHealthy excitement: ordinary daily routines filled with God's great purpose, not self-hypnosis and not an act.
In plain words

The first mark is excitement, and Pastor Sam's tagline for it is “Vision awakens our passion” (1:03:00). He explains it in Indonesian: a vision from God awakens our passion (gairah) in Him. He defines excitement generously: joy, cheerfulness, a strong feeling of happiness, enthusiasm, even a degree of commotion and stir (kehebohan, kegemparan) (1:03:08 to 1:03:20). And he removes an objection before it is raised. Excitement is not only for sanguine people or for extroverts. God gives excitement to everyone, whatever their personality type. People often box themselves in, “I'm an introvert, I'm an extrovert”, but this is something general in Scripture (1:02:32 to 1:02:56).

Next he separates two kinds of excitement. When someone close to God receives a vision, the excitement is not merely an emotional response to an opportunity: “I have the chance to get Hebron, to be blessed, to get a promotion.” There is nothing wrong with that, but it is not only that. Caleb knew there was a call of God in Hebron: “It's not just a city”, not just a promotion. What he pursued was God's call. The reason this matters is that following God's call requires staying close to God. Without that, people fall into a “default mode”: they follow their own instincts and their own call (1:03:46 to 1:04:39).

The evidence he gives runs in two directions. Scripture shows excitement in God's people: the psalmist glad to go to the house of the LORD, faces radiant from looking to God, the freedom of the Spirit that shows on the face, Moses at 120 still walking “with a spring in his step”. And it shows what happens when excitement is lost: Elijah sitting in the wilderness wanting to die, and David, who as a youth ran at Goliath, staying home in later years while his army went to war, and falling into sin out of boredom. From both directions he concludes that excitement is not a matter of temperament or performance. It comes “from inside out”, from understanding God's purpose and being guided by His word, and it turns ordinary routines into service of the vision God has given.

His definition

  • Tagline: “Vision awakens our passion” (1:03:00). Indonesian: Visi dari Tuhan itu akan membangkitkan yang namanya kita punya gairah di dalam dia.
  • Meaning: joy, cheerfulness, a strong feeling of happiness, enthusiasm, even a degree of commotion (kegembiraan, keriangan, perasaan senang yang kuat, antusiasme, kehebohan, kegemparan).
  • For everyone: not tied to being sanguine, extrovert or introvert (1:02:32, 1:06:29, 1:08:58).
  • Its source: God's call, not only an opportunity; “It's not just a city” (1:04:15).
  • Its direction: “not from outside in, but from inside out” (bukan dari luar ke dalam, tapi dari dalam ke luar, 1:07:52).

Three illustrations from life

He makes the idea concrete with three short pictures. First, the worship leader that morning, Ruth, is full of excitement; at the office everyone knows she has arrived while she is still on the ground floor, because she shouts a greeting from the ground floor (1:03:22 to 1:03:41). Second, it is wonderful to travel with someone full of excitement: “Come on, come on, don't just stay here, don't just stay at the hotel, get out of the car”, even when it is hot or raining. Such a person is not only cheerful when things are going well; they bring something new (1:04:42 to 1:05:09).

Third, he plays the opposite for laughs. Imagine him arriving every Sunday without excitement: “Guys, happy Sunday... Here we go again... Another Sunday... Why does Sunday come so fast... Well, thanks for coming... Today I don't know what I'll preach, but I'll try...” Some would even say, “Why am I here?” (1:05:11 to 1:05:45). The comedy makes the point: a preacher without excitement would empty the service of meaning, and so would a worshipper.

Do not drag your feet

This leads into a personal aside. What irritates him most is seeing young people drag their feet: “your life seems so heavy, so young already” (1:09:55 to 1:10:04). He often tells restaurant waiters, he admits, though his wife does not know, since she dislikes him correcting people, that if they want a long career in any field they should not drag their feet when they walk (1:10:07 to 1:10:26). He adds a joke on Scripture: “The word of God says the dragging of the righteous is led by the Lord... no: the steps of the righteous are led by the Lord” (1:10:30 to 1:10:39). He tells his own child the same: do not drag your feet; you have a spine, stand upright. The congregation is told to tell their neighbours, “He's not fierce, he's just like that” (1:10:41 to 1:10:55).

The joke plays on a familiar verse, here cited in passing (Psalm 37:23, ESV: “The steps of a man are established by the LORD, when he delights in his way;”). His wording is closest to the New King James Version (NKJV): “The steps of a good man are ordered by the LORD”. In the psalm, “steps” means the course of a life; he uses it lightly, for the wordplay.

Healthy excitement in ordinary life

He lightens the mood once more: be excited even when ordering food in a restaurant, “What's this, miss? What's this?”, until the waitress wonders whether this man is hungry or malfunctioning; then, to online viewers, an apology that the joke was meant for the second service (1:13:00 to 1:13:20). The serious point follows. For God's people, healthy excitement comes from doing ordinary daily routines, waking up, eating breakfast, going to the office, serving our families, filled with God's great purpose: “I am not just doing this and that... I am serving the visions and dreams God has given” (1:13:22 to 1:14:06).

He has the congregation say to their neighbours, “Fill yourself with excitement” and “Be excited about life”, and immediately rules out the shallow reading: this is not self-hypnosis, “positive, positive”. “No, no, no. This is from inside out.” It comes from understanding God's purpose and being guided by God's word, and from that come vision and planning (1:14:12 to 1:14:38).

The reasoning, step by step

  1. PremiseA vision from God awakens passion, and God gives this to every personality type.
  2. DistinctionThis passion responds to God's call, not only to an opportunity, so it keeps a person close to God instead of in “default mode”.
  3. MechanismTurning to the Lord removes the veil and brings freedom; freedom brings joy, peace and excitement, which show on the face (2 Corinthians 3:16-18; Psalm 34:5).
  4. EvidencePsalm 122:1, Moses at 120, Joseph in prison and young David show it; Elijah and the older David show what its absence leads to.
  5. ConclusionExcitement must come from the inside out, from God's purpose and word, and it fills ordinary routines with meaning.

Part C · Point 2 · Expectation

Expectation: “Vision sustains our resilience”

ProblemExcitement alone fades or scatters: cheerful people may have no plan, and people with big visions may say “could that really happen?”
Big ideaExpectation keeps yesterday's promise relevant to today's obedience, and it trusts God's character rather than demanding that every dream come true.
How (tools)Caleb's 45 years and his tenacity; Psalm 27:13-14; his father's house church and sermon notes; his son's proposal; marriage, family meals and hiking; Moses who saw from afar.
ResultA hope that does not drop, whether or not the believer sees the fulfilment, because “God is faithful” and the journey matters more than the reward.
In plain words

The second mark grows out of the first. “If a person has no excitement, he has no expectation” (1:14:39 to 1:14:44). Or, if he has a hope, he says it can no longer be fulfilled; he can draw up big visions, but then says, “Ah, could that really happen?” (1:14:45 to 1:14:56). So excitement is the soil in which expectation grows. Pastor Sam's tagline is “Vision sustains our resilience”, which he renders as vision supporting and upholding our tenacity (kegigihan). We are tenacious not only because we are diligent, but because there is “the purpose of God” (1:14:58 to 1:15:11).

But the relation runs one way only. Excitement is needed for expectation, yet “excitement alone is not enough” (excitement saja tidak cukup, 1:15:59). Many people are “cheerful, cheerful, cheerful” but have no expectation: they cannot write a plan, cannot organise, their own calendar is a mess, their schedules exist but are scattered. They are very positive, full of faith, but their expectation cannot be seen or read by anyone else (1:16:01 to 1:16:33). Caleb was different. At 85 he said, “I am still strong”, as strong as 45 years before: still expectant, still full of hope, still full of vision, still tenacious (1:16:39 to 1:16:55).

From Caleb he draws the line he repeats three times: “Expectation is what keeps yesterday's promise relevant to today's obedience” (1:16:56). The promise was received in the past; while it is not yet fulfilled, the believer must still obey today, and the reason is expectation that God will do great things. Then he guards the idea against misuse. “Expectation is not assuming God will fulfill every dream. It is trusting his character while pursuing purposes in obedience” (1:25:57 to 1:26:15). Some dreams are fulfilled, as Caleb's was; some are not, as Moses' was not. “That's fine. Isn't the journey more important than the reward?”

His definitions

  • Tagline: “Vision sustains our resilience” (1:14:58). Indonesian: Visi itu akan mendukung menopang kegigihan kita.
  • Its root: “We are tenacious because there is the purpose of God” (kita gigih ini karena ada the purpose of God, 1:15:11), not only because we are diligent.
  • Its link to excitement: without excitement there is no expectation, but “excitement alone is not enough” (1:15:59).
  • Key line: “Expectation is what keeps yesterday's promise relevant to today's obedience” (1:16:56, said three times). Indonesian: Expectation adalah yang membuat janji yang kita terima di hari kemarin masih nyambung kalau kita mau patuh hari ini.
  • Guardrail: “Expectation is not assuming God will fulfill every dream. It is trusting his character while pursuing purposes in obedience” (1:25:57 to 1:26:15). Indonesian: Percaya karakternya Tuhan sambil mengejar panggilan Tuhan dengan kepatuhan.
  • Its limit: whether we actually see it “is God's business”, but “expectation must not drop” (yang namanya ekspektasi tidak boleh turun, 1:17:56).

Caleb's tenacity

To show what vision-sustained tenacity looks like, he points to Caleb's background. Caleb, he says, “was actually not a Hebrew”: he was a Kenizzite, “of the same tribe as Moses' father-in-law”, adopted by the Hebrews, who chose to follow them. He could have said, “These people are all stubborn, they won't enter the promised land”, and simply left. But he did not. He persevered because, in effect, he said, “I am not only serving you; I am serving God's purpose in my life” (1:15:14 to 1:15:47). When he saw God, he wanted to do something.

Reading it carefully: He says Caleb was “orang Keni namanya orang Kenas” (1:15:20), joining two names, and links him with Moses' father-in-law. The texts keep the two groups apart. Caleb is “the Kenizzite”, in Indonesian orang Kenas (Joshua 14:6, 14; Numbers 32:12). Moses' father-in-law's family were Kenites, in Indonesian orang Keni: “the descendants of the Kenite, Moses' father-in-law, went up with the people of Judah” (Judges 1:16, ESV). Genesis lists them side by side as different peoples: “the land of the Kenites, the Kenizzites, the Kadmonites” (Genesis 15:19, ESV). So “same tribe as Moses' father-in-law” is not supported. That Caleb's clan was not of Israelite descent and was absorbed into Judah is a widely held reading of “the Kenizzite”, though the text never says “adopted”, and it also presents Caleb as Judah's own leader (“from the tribe of Judah, Caleb the son of Jephunneh”, Numbers 13:6, ESV). His point, that Caleb kept serving God's purpose among a stubborn people when he could have walked away, does not depend on the detail.

Joshua 14 supplies the words he leans on, “I am still strong” (1:16:39), quoted here in the English Standard Version (ESV) and the Indonesian Terjemahan Baru (TB):

10And now, behold, the LORD has kept me alive, just as he said, these forty-five years since the time that the LORD spoke this word to Moses, while Israel walked in the wilderness. His father's house church (his story)

The longest illustration in the sermon is about his own father. His father came to faith (bertobat) at 50, and a few months later opened his home for worship. On Sundays many people helped, and young people came early to set up the chairs. But on Wednesdays, for the 25 years he watched while living at home, until his father was 75, it was his father who set up the chairs (1:18:26 to 1:18:59). He interrupts himself with a joke at his own expense: “Don't look at me like that, I was at school till 3”; “ungrateful son, didn't help” (1:19:00 to 1:19:05).

From one or two in the afternoon, full of excitement and full of expectation, his father set up the chairs. When the son came home from school before the house meeting, his father would be praying over the chairs and over people's names. From Monday he began phoning people; from Tuesday he visited all the neighbours. And he always said the same thing: “Someone will surely be baptized, someone will surely believe in God.” At first the son did not believe it. But he saw with his own eyes his neighbours, relatives and kin, one by one, receive the Lord Jesus and be baptized (1:19:10 to 1:19:58).

Later his father was old and retired; at 75 he could no longer go anywhere (he died at 86). But he knew his son often went to minister in many places, and he always said, “Surely many will repent, surely many will be baptized.” He could not go, but he passed his excitement on to his son (1:20:01 to 1:20:22). A few weeks before the sermon, clearing his parents' house after his mother died, Pastor Sam found his father's sermon notes. Up to two weeks before his death his father had written a sermon almost every day, as if the Wednesday service were still running and he would preach every week. When he phoned, he would say, “Papa got a great sermon”, tell it at length, and ask his son to preach it if he had the chance. “Still writing sermon”, every day, before he died (1:20:23 to 1:21:20). Pastor Sam prays that each of his hearers will still glorify God to their last breath.

He has the congregation say “Expect something” and adds that family life must be full of hope (1:21:53 to 1:21:56). The reasoning, step by step

  1. DependenceWithout excitement there is no expectation; with excitement alone there may be no plan and no visible hope.
  2. SourceVision sustains tenacity because it rests on God's purpose, as Caleb served God's purpose among a stubborn people.
  3. DefinitionExpectation keeps a past promise connected to present obedience: while a promise waits, we still obey, because we expect God to act.
  4. EvidenceCaleb at 85, Psalm 27:13-14, his father's chairs and sermons, and his own family life show expectation at work.
  5. GuardrailExpectation is not a claim on God to fulfil every dream; it trusts God's character. Caleb received his promise and Moses did not, and both fit.
  6. ConclusionSeeing the outcome is God's business; keeping expectation is ours, and it must not drop.

Part C · Point 3 · Experience

Experience: “Vision inspires our participation”

ProblemHowever great the excitement and however large the expectation, nothing happens if it is not worked on.
Big ideaA vision from God moves us to take part: Caleb asked for the mountain knowing he would have to fight for it.
How (tools)Joshua 14:11-12: “going out and coming in”, and “give me this hill country”; the contrast with other mountains; Moses and Caleb.
ResultParticipation in both prayer and action, contentment with the mountain God promised, and the recognition of God's work through our experiences.
In plain words

The third mark completes the sequence. Caleb had excitement and he had expectation; “only then will he undergo experience” (nah baru dia akan ngalamin experience, 1:26:37). The tagline is “Vision inspires our participation”, and in Indonesian he puts it as “he experienced it, he did it” (dia mengalami, dia lakukan, 1:26:44 to 1:26:46). The logic is blunt: however big the excitement, however big the expectation, “it will not happen if it is not worked on” (gak akan jadi kalau tidak digawein, 1:27:16). There must be experience; there must be our participation (peran serta kita).

His first evidence is in Joshua 14:11. At 85 Caleb could still “go out and come in”, and Pastor Sam gives the phrase two meanings: going out, to battle, beyond the city, and also going in and out of the tabernacle. Caleb could still pray and still fight (1:26:50 to 1:27:03). His second evidence is the request itself. “Give me that mountain”: Caleb knew that afterwards he would have to fight. He knew there was God's promise, but he also knew he had to defeat the giants, the Anakim; he had to work and struggle (1:27:25 to 1:27:37).

Then he adds a quality that participation produces. Someone who walks with God, enjoys being near Him, has expectation in Him and is willing to act will not envy other people's blessings. Caleb did not ask for a mountain someone else might have; he did not ask for the highest mountain, Hermon; he did not want another valley or a river. “If God gives me Hebron, that's more than enough; what matters is that I experience the journey with God.” He was secure in God: never mind who has that mountain or the one over there; “I was given Hebron and I will fight for Hebron.” So: “He asked for the mountain God had promised.” Make sure you pursue the right mountain, something that is truly your portion in this life (1:27:50 to 1:28:59).

His definition

  • Tagline: “Vision inspires our participation” (1:26:44). Indonesian: Dia mengalami, dia lakukan; harus ada peran serta kita (1:27:20).
  • Order: excitement and expectation first; “only then” experience (1:26:37).
  • Necessity: “It will not happen if it is not worked on” (1:27:16); dreams, vision, zeal and big expectation are good, “but they will not happen if they are not done” (1:27:40 to 1:27:48).
  • Two sides: still able to fight and still able to pray (1:27:03).
  • Aim: “He asked for the mountain God had promised” (1:28:45). Indonesian: Pastikan Anda mengejar gunung yang benar... sesuatu yang memang porsinya kita.
  • Fruit: “Recognize God's work through your experiences” (1:30:51). Indonesian: Mengenali rancangan Tuhan melalui pengalaman-pengalaman kehidupan kita.

Going out and coming in

The phrase comes from Joshua 14:11, quoted here in the English Standard Version (ESV) and the Indonesian Terjemahan Baru (TB):

11I am still as strong today as I was in the day that Moses sent me; my strength now is as my strength was then, for war and for going and coming.

Joshua 14:11 · ESV

11pada waktu ini aku masih sama kuat seperti pada waktu aku disuruh Musa; seperti kekuatanku pada waktu itu demikianlah kekuatanku sekarang untuk berperang dan untuk keluar masuk.

Yosua 14:11 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)
Reading it carefully: He gives “going out and coming in” (TB keluar masuk) two meanings: going out to battle, and going in and out of the tabernacle to pray (1:26:54 to 1:27:03). Elsewhere the phrase means active leadership, above all leading people out to battle and back: Moses asks for a leader “who shall go out before them and come in before them” (Numbers 27:17, ESV), and at 120 Moses says, “I am no longer able to go out and come in” (Deuteronomy 31:2, ESV). In Joshua 14:11 it stands beside “for war”, so the first meaning fits the verse well. The tabernacle meaning is not what the idiom means here; it works as his application, that Caleb's strength served prayer as well as battle.

Give me that mountain

12So now give me this hill country of which the LORD spoke on that day, for you heard on that day how the Anakim were there, with great fortified cities. It may be that the LORD will be with me, and I shall drive them out just as the LORD said.”

Joshua 14:12 · ESV

12Oleh sebab itu, berikanlah kepadaku pegunungan, yang dijanjikan TUHAN pada waktu itu, sebab engkau sendiri mendengar pada waktu itu, bahwa di sana ada orang Enak dengan kota-kota yang besar dan berkubu. Mungkin TUHAN menyertai aku, sehingga aku menghalau mereka, seperti yang difirmankan TUHAN.”

Yosua 14:12 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)

Two things in the verse carry his argument. First, Caleb asks for “this hill country of which the LORD spoke”: not any mountain, but the promised one. Second, he names the Anakim and their fortified cities in the same breath: he asks for the place knowing that receiving it means a fight. Promise and effort come together. The book of Joshua records that the fight did happen:

13According to the commandment of the LORD to Joshua, he gave to Caleb the son of Jephunneh a portion among the people of Judah, Kiriath-arba, that is, Hebron (Arba was the father of Anak). 14And Caleb drove out from there the three sons of Anak, Sheshai and Ahiman and Talmai, the descendants of Anak.

Joshua 15:13-14 · ESV
Reading it carefully: Hermon does not appear in Joshua 14; setting it beside Hebron is his illustration of a mountain Caleb did not envy. Hermon is indeed the high mountain at the far northern edge of the conquered land (“from the Valley of the Arnon to Mount Hermon”, Deuteronomy 3:8, ESV). His conclusion, that Caleb asked for the specific hill country “of which the LORD spoke”, is exactly what verse 12 says.

Secure in God, not envious

He reads Caleb's choice as a sign of security in God (rasa aman sama Tuhan, 1:28:33). A person who walks with God and is willing to act does not measure his portion against others'. What matters is not the size of the mountain but “that I experience the journey with God” (1:28:27 to 1:28:30). That is why the line he leaves with the congregation is “He asked for the mountain God had promised”, followed by the application: make sure you pursue the right mountain, something that is truly your portion in this life (1:28:45 to 1:28:59).

Caleb got it, Moses did not

He sets the two men side by side one more time: “He got it, Moses didn't, it's okay.” What matters is seeing God, walking with God and experiencing God (1:29:01 to 1:29:04). This joins the third mark to the guardrail on expectation in the previous chapter: the reward is God's to give, while the journey with God is the believer's to walk.

The reasoning, step by step

  1. OrderExcitement and expectation come first; experience follows from them.
  2. NecessityWithout action, even great excitement and expectation produce nothing.
  3. EvidenceAt 85 Caleb could still go out and come in, and he asked for a mountain he knew he would have to fight for.
  4. AimHe asked for the mountain God had promised, not the highest or anyone else's, because he was secure in God.
  5. ConclusionParticipation is how a believer experiences God and recognizes His work in life; whether the reward comes now or later, the journey with God is what matters.

Synthesis · How the sermon fits together

One spark, three marks, two generations

ProblemThe three marks could be heard as three separate tips, and Moses' unfulfilled dream could seem to undo the promise of a lasting spark.
Big ideaThe marks form one chain, and Moses and Caleb together show that God's promises cross generations, so the spark is an inheritance to pass on.
How (tools)His own links between the marks; Moses and Caleb side by side; what to pass on to children; his closing line, questions and prayer.
ResultA believer who keeps excitement, expectation and experience, and hands all three to the next generation, recognizes God's work in a whole life.
In plain words

Pastor Sam ties the three marks together himself, at the joins between them. Without excitement there is no expectation (1:14:39); excitement alone is not enough (1:15:59); with excitement and expectation in place, “only then” comes experience (1:26:37); and however big the first two are, nothing happens unless it is done (1:27:16). So the marks are not a menu. Each one needs the one before it, and the last is where the first two become real. All three hang from the same source, a vision from God, which is why each tagline begins with “Vision”.

The sermon then answers an objection it raised itself. If expectation means “I will see the goodness of the LORD”, what of Moses, who did not enter the land? He answers twice. First, expectation is not a claim that God must fulfil every dream; it trusts God's character, and so “some dreams are fulfilled, like Caleb's; some are not, like Moses'. That's fine” (1:26:22). Second, Moses' dream was not lost. Caleb was not Moses' son, but he was, in Pastor Sam's word, Moses' disciple, and what Moses did not receive, Caleb did. So “God's promises cross generations” (janji Tuhan itu lintas generasi, 1:29:23). If this generation, full of excitement and pure expectation and doing whatever needs doing, does not receive it, the next generation will.

That conclusion turns the sermon towards children. The inheritance that matters is not only “this and that”, though there is nothing wrong with that, but the spark itself: excitement in the Lord, expectation that trusts Him, and the willingness to fight. His own father, who set up the chairs every Wednesday and wrote sermons until two weeks before he died, is the example: “he couldn't go, but he passed his excitement on to me” (1:20:15 to 1:20:17). The closing line, “Recognize God's work through your experiences”, gathers everything into one habit of mind.

Moses and Caleb together

The two men appear at every stage of the sermon, and each time they answer a different question. Under excitement, Moses at 120 shows that the spark need not fade with age: “he still walked with a spring in his step”. Under expectation, Moses shows the limit of what a believer can claim: he saw the land from afar and did not enter, and that is “fine”, because expectation trusts God's character rather than guaranteeing outcomes. Caleb shows the other side: he waited and received, proving that “God is faithful”. Under experience, the two together show what matters most: “He got it, Moses didn't, it's okay. What matters is seeing God, walking with God and experiencing God” (1:29:01 to 1:29:04).

From the pair he draws the sermon's widest conclusion. Although Caleb was not Moses' biological son, he was Moses' disciple; Moses did not receive the land, Caleb did; therefore God's promises cross generations (1:29:12 to 1:29:23). He presents this as his conclusion from the story, and the texts give it a foothold: the promise to Caleb includes his children (“an inheritance for you and your children forever”, Joshua 14:9, English Standard Version, ESV), and the first promise in Numbers already names his descendants:

24But my servant Caleb, because he has a different spirit and has followed me fully, I will bring into the land into which he went, and his descendants shall possess it.

Numbers 14:24 · ESV

24Tetapi hamba-Ku Kaleb, karena lain jiwa yang ada padanya dan ia mengikut Aku dengan sepenuhnya, akan Kubawa masuk ke negeri yang telah dimasukinya itu, dan keturunannya akan memilikinya.

Bilangan 14:24 · TB (Terjemahan Baru)
Reading it carefully: Scripture does not call Caleb Moses' disciple; the one named as Moses' assistant is Joshua (Exodus 24:13; 33:11; Numbers 11:28). Caleb was sent by Moses (“Moses the servant of the LORD sent me”, Joshua 14:7, ESV) and served under him, so “a man formed under Moses” is fair. His conclusion that promises cross generations does not depend on the word “disciple”: it rests on the promise to Caleb's descendants and on God's word to Moses, “I will give it to your offspring” (Deuteronomy 34:4, ESV).

What to pass on to children

“What inheritance do they get?” (1:29:43). Not only “this and that”, though nothing is wrong with that. He lists three things, one for each mark (1:29:51 to 1:30:48):

  • Excitement in the Lord: say to our children, “I was glad when they said to me, let us go to the house of the Lord” (Psalm 122:1); say “Papa is going to work, pray for me: today is gonna be a great day” (hari ini akan jadi hari yang luar biasa), so that the child says it too.
  • Expectation: teach them to rely on the Lord and to pray “Lord, if you are willing” (Tuhan jika engkau berkenan): if you allow it, open this door; if you do not, close it.
  • Experience: teach them to fight, to do whatever is needed.

“I feel that is an important inheritance for our children” (1:30:48). The prayer he teaches echoes a verse he does not cite, quoted here for reference (James 4:15, ESV: “Instead you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that.’”); even his “this and that” (ini dan itu) matches it.

The closing line

“Recognize God's work through your experiences” (1:30:51), which he renders mengenali rancangan Tuhan melalui pengalaman-pengalaman kehidupan kita, recognizing God's plan through the experiences of our lives. It is the end point of the chain: a vision awakens passion, sustains hope, inspires action, and in that action the believer comes to see what God has been doing.

To take home

He closes with three questions for the congregation to carry home (1:31:01 to 1:31:37). They are his reflection prompts, given without answers.

  1. What still ignites my passion for God?Apa sih yang masih membuat saya menyala? He prays that his hearers are still on fire for their families and for God.
  2. What am I looking forward to with faith?Apa sih yang masih saya harapkan, yang masih saya doakan, yang masih saya punya ekspektasi yang besar? What am I still hoping for, still praying for, still holding a great expectation for?
  3. What step of faith am I ready to take?Given in Indonesian only: Langkah iman apa yang saya siap untuk ambil?

The congregation stands to sing “How Great Is Our God”.
